No Skill for Deciding

Research software, documented indecision, and what structure is actually for

  • Indecision is cheap. Until it isn't.
  • What structure is actually for
  • Why there's no skill for this

Lately I've been seeing AI skills for almost everything, from reverse-engineering design systems to building interfaces and running browser tests. I've tried several and written a few of my own, but the skill I actually want borders on philosophy: a universal recipe for making any research software project manageable.

The trouble is that every project has its own quirks: different timelines, people, politics, expectations, and definitions of success. Over the past few years, I've grown genuinely interested in technical project management, and being something of a control freak, I've tried increasingly hard to bring order to the chaos through meeting notes, checklists, issue trackers, documentation, and now AI.

Yet the more I organize, the less convinced I am that organization is the problem.

We've become very good at documenting indecision

Most of these tools help us collect signals about a project, but having more information doesn't necessarily bring us closer to making a decision.

I've sat through plenty of meetings where people debated technical approaches for hours, quoted documentation, compared examples, and carefully recorded everything in the notes, only to leave without a decision, an action item, or even a plan to revisit the question. We had a beautiful record of everything we'd discussed and nothing about what we were going to do.

We often call this collaboration when we're actually avoiding decisions.

And I understand why. Making a decision means ruling out alternatives, risking disagreement, and accepting the possibility of being wrong. Keeping things open is much more comfortable because everyone gets to contribute without anyone having to commit.

Indecision is cheap. Until it isn't.

Not deciding costs very little right now, and research software makes it particularly easy to postpone the consequences. Academic research follows strange cycles. Scientific questions evolve, requirements change, and funding comes and goes, leaving long stretches where nobody is quite sure what the final product should look like. Another discussion or another round of exploration can feel perfectly reasonable, until a grant deliverable, conference demonstration, or release deadline suddenly forces our hand.

All those choices we've comfortably postponed now need to be made at once, with fewer options and less time to consider them. We were always going to have to make those decisions; postponing them didn't avoid the cost, it gave up our freedom to choose when and how to pay it.

Even a bad decision gives us something concrete to challenge, test, or reverse. An unmade decision can linger indefinitely, disguised as an open question when it's really a choice nobody wanted to make.

What structure is actually for

I'm starting to think the structure I've been looking for shouldn't try to force every project into the same workflow. Its job is much simpler: make inaction visible and consequential.

Before another discussion, name the decision we're trying to make and who has the authority to make it. If we're not ready, agree on what we need to learn, who will find out, and when we'll return to the question. Most importantly, establish what happens if nobody makes the call, whether that means dropping the feature, keeping the existing approach, or stopping the work.

Doing nothing is a legitimate decision, but we should make it deliberately rather than drift into it.

Why there's no skill for this

I still want that universal recipe, but perhaps I've been asking tooling to solve something it cannot. AI can summarize discussions, compare options, and recommend a direction, but it can't take responsibility for the choices we make.

The quirks of research software projects aren't going away, and I can't standardize them out of existence. What I can standardize is how we handle decisions, including the decision to wait or do nothing.

We don't need another tool to keep our options open. We need the discipline to close some of them.
